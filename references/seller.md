# Seller mode — win without gutting margin

Read at stages 6–7 when the user is issuing the quote.

---

## The premise most sellers get wrong

**You usually lose on comparability, not on price.**

A buyer levelling three quotes reaches for the one that is easiest to trust. A quote that
is hard to compare gets marked up in the buyer's head for uncertainty, and that
uncertainty premium is invisible to you — you just lose and assume you were expensive.

So the first question is never "is my price too high". It is: **what will this quote look
like on the buyer's levelling sheet?** Run the buyer's normalization against your own
quote. Every line that would be quarantined, flagged low-confidence, or marked "excluded"
is a place where you are being silently penalised.

---

## Competitiveness read

Where a competitor quote or a prior award is available, run the full pipeline and report:

```
POSITION: [rank] on normalized basis, [rank] on headline
Gap to leader: [amount, %] — of which [amount] is scope, [amount] is rate
Winnable: [yes / no / only with structural change]
```

Where no competitor quote exists, say so and switch to **AUDIT**: assess the quote on its
own merits against the buyer's likely levelling process. Do not invent a competitive
landscape.

---

## Diagnosing your own quote

Run these seven checks before touching price.

1. **Exclusion count.** Every exclusion is a buyer objection you created. Which could you
   absorb cheaply and price into the base? Cheap-to-you, expensive-to-them items are the
   highest-leverage inclusions in existence.
2. **Ambiguity audit.** Find every "as per standard", "to be confirmed", "as required".
   Each one either becomes a specific commitment or becomes a reason to discount you.
3. **Comparability.** Does your line structure match how the buyer asked? A quote that
   does not map to the buyer's BOQ or RFP structure forces them to do work, and buyers
   penalise work.
4. **Unbalanced rates.** Do you have lines conspicuously above market? They will be found.
   One outlier rate discredits the whole sheet.
5. **Front-loading.** Advance-heavy terms read as a cash-flow problem. If you need the
   cash, price the alternative so the buyer sees the trade rather than the demand.
6. **Justification density.** Does the quote explain *why* anything costs what it costs?
   An unexplained number is compared; an explained one is evaluated. This is the single
   cheapest change available.
7. **Validity window.** Too short reads as pressure. Too long is free optionality you gave
   away. State the reason for the window you chose.

## The nine restructuring moves — before discounting

Ordered by margin preserved.

1. **Justify, don't drop.** Add cost build-up for the top three lines by value. Frequently
   ends the price objection without moving the price at all.
2. **Unbundle.** Split the monolith into base + options. The headline falls, the buyer
   chooses up, and you stop losing on a number that included things they never wanted.
3. **Tier.** Good / better / best. Anchors the middle, and converts a
   yes/no decision into a which-one decision. Different question, better odds.
4. **Re-spec.** Offer a genuine lower-spec alternative *alongside* the original, with the
   difference stated plainly. Never substitute silently — that is how you lose the account
   on delivery rather than at quote stage.
5. **Trade terms for price.** Faster payment, longer commitment, deposit, or a multi-site
   commitment in exchange for the rate. You are selling certainty, which is worth money.
6. **Add cover instead of cutting price.** Extended warranty, extra support, training,
   spares. Costs you less than the equivalent discount and increases switching cost.
7. **Re-price risk.** If the buyer wants an uncapped liability or punitive LDs, price it
   and show the line. Buyers routinely drop the clause when they see its cost.
8. **Volume or term ladder.** Publish the break table. It converts "too expensive" into
   "how do we reach the next tier" — a conversation you win.
9. **Walk.** Below your margin floor, losing is the correct outcome. A contract won below
   cost is a liability that occupies capacity you need for profitable work.

**Discounting is the tenth move, not the first.** A discount given without a structural
change resets the buyer's reference price permanently, and every future quote from you is
measured against the discounted one.

---

## The margin floor

Before any restructuring, state the floor explicitly:

1. Direct cost + attributable overhead = the true floor.
2. Opportunity cost of capacity consumed.
3. Strategic value, if any — logo, reference, entry into an account — **quantified with a
   time limit**, not asserted forever. "Strategic" without a number is how firms lose money
   deliberately for years.

Where the user has not supplied cost data, you cannot compute a floor. Say that, and ask.
Do not estimate someone else's cost base.

---

## The restructured quote

When producing it:

1. Keep the buyer's structure if they gave one. Their format, their order, their numbering.
2. Lead with scope, then price. A price read before the scope is read is always too high.
3. State inclusions **and** exclusions explicitly. A quote with no exclusions list reads
   as either naive or evasive, and buyers assume the latter.
4. One assumptions register, visible. Assumptions in a register are professional;
   assumptions discovered during delivery are disputes.
5. Every price has a basis: quantity × rate, or a build-up, or a stated lump-sum reason.
6. Options priced separately and clearly marked optional, so the base number stays clean.

---

## Honest counsel

When the user's quote is genuinely competitive and they are about to discount out of
nerves, **say so and say don't**. That is the single highest-value seller-side output this
skill produces, and it only works if the skill is equally willing to say the opposite.
